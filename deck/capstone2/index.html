The business problem

Solar is the part of tomorrow's plan that depends on the weather

What CAISO does

Locks in tomorrow's power plan today

Bids close at 10 a.m. Pacific. The plan is published at about 1 p.m. It says which plants run tomorrow and how much each makes.

What goes wrong

The solar forecast misses

Too high, and operators buy backup power at the last minute. Too low, and they hold reserves they never use.

What we do

Forecast solar output a day ahead

Hour by hour, using only what is known at 10 a.m. We then compare ourselves with CAISO's own forecast.

CAISO runs California's main power grid. It locks in tomorrow's plan today: bids close at 10 a.m. Pacific and the plan comes out around 1 p.m. Solar is a big part of that plan, and it depends on tomorrow's weather. When the solar forecast is wrong, somebody pays: either backup power bought at the last minute, or reserves held for nothing. Our project is a better day-ahead solar forecast.

Our target variable Hourly solar output, in megawatts (MW)

How much power utility-scale solar plants in the CAISO area actually produce each hour, as measured by the U.S. Energy Information Administration.

Net load definition: U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA).

A fair question last time was why we are not forecasting energy use. Demand matters, but CAISO already forecasts it. What the grid operator plans around is net load: demand minus what wind and solar supply. Solar is the weather-driven part of that equation, so a better solar forecast changes how much gas, battery or import power has to be lined up. Our target variable is the hourly output of utility-scale solar in the CAISO area, in megawatts, as measured by the EIA.

The rule that keeps the test honest

We may only use what is known at 10 a.m.

Allowed (main model) Weather forecast made two days before

Published before the 10 a.m. bid close for every hour of the next day. This is the input we use.

Comparison only Weather forecast made one day before

For afternoon hours it is likely published after the bid close. We run it only as a labeled comparison, never as our result.

Why it matters: a forecast that peeks at later information looks better than it could be in real life.

Both versions are in the data, so the comparison is a single switch.

CAISO closes bids at 10 a.m. Pacific the day before. Any input we use has to be available by then. Weather models publish a few hours after they start running, so a forecast made one day before the target hour may arrive after the deadline for afternoon hours. Our main model therefore uses the forecast made two days before, and the one-day-before version is shown only as a clearly labeled comparison. This is the average solar day, by season. Output starts around 6 a.m., peaks at midday and is finished by about 8 p.m. in every season. In summer the midday peak averages about 17,400 megawatts. In winter it is about 11,900, roughly a third lower. The shape is very regular, which is good news. The hard part is not the shape, it is how much of that shape the weather gives us on a particular day. For the model, that means hour of day and season have to be inputs.

Midday = 11 a.m. to 2 p.m. Pacific. Cloud cover is the forecast made two days earlier. Includes all seasons, so winter's lower sun also plays a part. Sources: EIA, Open-Meteo.

Here is why weather is worth forecasting. We grouped midday hours by how cloudy the forecast said it would be. On clear days, solar delivers about 68 percent of installed capacity at midday. On very cloudy days it is about 41 percent. Part of that gap is winter sun, which is lower, but the link is strong. Across more than nine thousand daylight hours, the forecast sunshine and the actual output move together with a correlation of 0.87. And the weather can change fast: on October 10 of this year, output fell by 55 percent from one day to the next. That is the kind of day that costs a grid operator money.

Why it mattersErrors must be scaled by capacity

A 1,000 MW miss meant more in 2024 than it will in 2026. We report errors as a share of installed capacity.

Why it mattersTurned-down solar hides true output

In April 2026 about 1 in 4 possible megawatt-hours was turned down. Over the whole period it is 7.6%.

Sources: EIA Form 860M (capacity, includes about 2.8 GW in Arizona and Nevada); CAISO curtailment reports.

Two patterns the model has to live with. On the left, installed solar capacity grew from about 21.0 to 26.0 gigawatts, a 24 percent increase in under three years. So we cannot compare raw megawatt errors across years; we express every error as a share of installed capacity. On the right, curtailment. This is solar that plants could have produced but the grid turned down, usually in spring when it is sunny and demand is low. In April 2026 about one in four possible megawatt-hours was turned down. Across the whole period it is 7.6 percent. Because the measured output is lower on those days, we flag those hours and report results with and without them.
Data understanding

Four data problems, and what we do about each

IssueWhat we foundWhat we do
Clocks disagreeEIA reports in UTC. CAISO files use the local clock, which shifts with daylight saving time.Convert every source to one clock (hour ending, UTC) and check against sunrise and sunset.
Missing hours225 of 22,896 hours (under 1%): weather 129, CAISO forecast 72, EIA 24.Fill short gaps, drop long ones, and say which.
Turned-down solar7.6% of possible solar energy over the period. Up to 24% in a spring month.Flag those hours and report results with and without them.

Also noted: tiny negative readings at night (about 40 MW below zero, against peaks of 17,000 MW). They are handled explicitly.

These are the four data problems we found, and each one has a written rule. Clocks: EIA is in UTC and CAISO files follow the local clock, so we convert everything to one clock and check it against sunrise and sunset. Missing hours: fewer than one percent, mostly in the weather feed. Turned-down solar: we flag it. More panels over time: we scale errors by installed capacity. One more small thing: at night the EIA series shows tiny negative values, about 40 megawatts, which is negligible next to 17,000 at midday but we treat explicitly.
Privacy and delivery risks

Low privacy risk, three delivery risks

Privacy: low

Public, system-wide data

No customers, households or personal information. The capacity file names power plants and companies, which are public records.

Open-Meteo requires credit (CC BY 4.0) and its free tier is non-commercial. EIA and CAISO data are public, and we cite them.

Delivery risk 1

Timing at 10 a.m.

A forecast is only useful if its inputs exist by the bid close. A late weather feed would break it.

We use the two-day-ahead forecast and check the one-day version only as a comparison.

Delivery risk 2 and 3

Limits and reproducibility

The free weather service caps daily calls. Our full pull used about 6,500 of the 10,000 allowed in a day.

Raw files are saved in the repository with a data dictionary, so nobody needs to re-pull.

The syllabus asks us to assess privacy and delivery risks as well as data quality. Privacy risk is low. All five sources are public and system-wide: no customers, no households, no personal information. The capacity file names power plants and companies, but those are public records. The licensing points are to credit Open-Meteo and to remember that its free tier is non-commercial. Delivery has three risks. First, timing: the inputs must exist by 10 a.m. Second, limits: the free weather service caps daily calls, and our full pull used about 6,500 of the 10,000 allowed in a day. Third, reproducibility: we saved the raw files and a data dictionary in the repository so the team never has to re-pull.

Before building any model we score the reference forecasts on our own data. Same-as-yesterday misses by 4.9 percent of installed capacity. CAISO's published day-ahead forecast misses by 5.7 percent, and it runs about 830 megawatts too high on average. Most of that bias disappears when we add back the solar that was turned down, and then CAISO's miss is 3.7 percent. Part of that is by construction, because CAISO defines curtailment against its own forecast, so we show both. Our target was to be within 1.25 times CAISO's miss. That is 4.6 percent or about 7 percent depending on which number counts. We will settle that before modeling and report both. One more detail: same-as-yesterday uses afternoon information that is not available at 10 a.m., and if we limit it to what is known then, it is 6.1 percent. We split in time order, never at random, because a forecast is always about the future. The earliest 80 percent of days, 763 of them, is for training and for four rolling checks. The latest 191 days, April 10 to October 17 of this year, is locked until the end and scored once. One honest limit: that last block runs from spring to mid-October, so it has no winter, and we judge winter accuracy in the validation checks instead.
