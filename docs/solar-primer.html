The 1-minute story

Electricity is like water in a bathtub.

  • Electricity is used at the moment it is made. Very little can be saved for later.
  • The water level must stay flat every second.
  • Solar is a tap that opens with the sun. Clouds squeeze it.
  • Solar is big: 25% of California's power in 2025. At noon on our 4 sample days, solar matched 78% to 95% of what CAISO's area was using.
  • The power plan is locked the day before: about 97% of tomorrow's power is lined up by 1 p.m. the day before. So we must guess tomorrow's sun today.

✓SOURCE 25%: California Energy Commission, 2025 ≈ROUGH ESTIMATE 78% to 95%: 4 sample days; CAISO’s solar file reads 12% to 13% above the U.S. Energy Information Administration ≈ROUGH ESTIMATE 97%: see section 7

Our business problem

Tomorrow's sun is the hardest part of tomorrow's power plan to know. The plan is locked at 10 a.m. the day before. A wrong guess costs money.

Our business question

Can we guess how much solar power California will make in each hour of tomorrow, using only what is known by 10 a.m. today, almost as well as the experts at CAISO (the California Independent System Operator, the grid referee)?

Our goal: our average miss is at most 1.25 times CAISO's average miss.

Who is CAISO, and why does it exist?

CAISO is short for the California Independent System Operator. Think of an airport. Many airlines own the planes (the power plants). Other companies own the runways (the wires). If everyone moved on their own, planes would crash. So 1 neutral air traffic controller tells everyone when and where to go. For California's power, that is CAISO.

  • Many companies make power. Many companies own the wires. Power cannot be stored in bulk, so supply must match use every second.
  • CAISO runs the market where power is offered and bought, and keeps the balance. It is a not-for-profit, independent of the companies that make and sell power.
  • Created by California's legislature in 1998, when the state opened electricity markets to competition. The Governor appoints its leaders.
  • Looks after about 80% of California's power.

The ruler: how we count electricity

Power is how fast. Energy is how much.

Think of a car. Speed is miles per hour. Distance is miles. Power is the speed of using electricity. Energy is the distance: how much was used over time.

Energy over a longer time

1 TWh (terawatt-hour) = 1,000,000 MWh. We use TWh for a whole year.

California uses 273 TWh per year. That is the same as 31 GW for 24 hours a day, 365 days a year (31 GW x 8,760 hours = 273 TWh).

✓SOURCE California Energy Commission, 2025 planning figure: 272,743 GWh =CALCULATED 272.7 TWh ÷ 8,760 hours = 31.1 GW

Our rule: 1 unit per job

  • An hour or a moment: MW. Whole state: GW.
  • A day or month: GWh. A year: TWh.
  • Homes: kW and kWh.
  • We never mix GWh and TWh in 1 table.
  • 1 MW for 1 hour = 1 MWh. So hourly MWh and MW are the same number.

✓SOURCE 1 MW of solar powers about 173 U.S. homes: Solar Energy Industries Association, national average through Q2 2022

Why does solar need a forecast?

The sun cannot be ordered around.

Solar is big and growing

  • 25% of California's 2025 mix: the 2nd biggest source, after natural gas (30%).
  • Solar capacity grew 24% from Mar 2024 to Sep 2026: 21.0 GW to 26.0 GW.

✓SOURCE California Energy Commission, 2025 ●OUR DATA monthly plant list from the U.S. Energy Information Administration, in our data

But it changes fast

  • A gas plant takes hours to warm up, like an oven.
  • So planners choose the day before which plants to start.
  • On Apr 15, 2025 solar fell from 14.1 GW at 5 p.m. to 0.9 GW at 7 p.m.: 13.3 GW gone in 2 hours.

✓SOURCE CAISO public daily file, Apr 15, 2025 =CALCULATED 14,148 MW minus 890 MW

  • Same sun, different seasons. Summer peaks near 17.4 GW. Winter peaks near 11.9 GW and the sun is gone by about 4 p.m.
  • That is why 1 forecast must learn all 4 seasons.

How power gets bought

A shared pool, not a 1-to-1 store.

Is it "buy and sell"? Yes, with 3 twists.
  1. It is a pool. Producers put power into a shared pool. Buyers take from it. Nobody learns whose power they got.
  2. The buyers are delivery companies and buying groups. Their job is to supply the homes on their wires.
  3. Most of the buying is done long before the day-ahead market. The market fills the gaps.

How to read this. About 87% of tomorrow's power is settled long before the market opens, by own plants and long-term contracts. But every solar farm still makes whatever the sun gives, however it was bought. So someone must guess solar for the whole grid. That guess is the guess we test against CAISO's own day-ahead solar forecast.

The clock: a rolling week

  • How far ahead? Up to 7 days. A seller can send an offer for next Thursday's power starting last Thursday.
  • Rolling. Every day, 1 window closes and a new window opens. So at any moment, 7 delivery days are open for offers.
  • Close: 10 a.m. the day before (Pacific clock). Between 10 a.m. and 1 p.m. no new offers are taken.
  • 1 p.m.: CAISO publishes the plan for every hour of tomorrow. This is the day-ahead market. ✓SOURCE CAISO market processes
  • Can offers change as the forecast changes? Yes, until the 10 a.m. close a seller can send a new offer. After the plan is published, that day-ahead deal is set. ≈ROUGH ESTIMATE educated guess: the CAISO rules we read do not say if a seller may edit an offer before the close
  • If an offer is not picked, it is no longer binding. The seller can offer again in the real-time market, at a different price. ✓SOURCE CAISO market rules, section 30.5.1
  • Can a buyer give some back? Not by cancelling. If a buyer ends up using less than its plan, the extra is sold back in the real-time market at the real-time price, which can be higher or lower. If it uses more, it buys the rest the same way. ✓SOURCE CAISO real-time load settlement paper

Who wins the match: the lowest price

  • CAISO lines up all offers from cheapest to most expensive. It picks the cheapest first, until there is enough power for everyone.
  • Everyone picked is paid the same price: the price of the last offer that was needed.
  • Solar and wind ask close to $0 per MWh because the sun and wind are free. So they go first and pull the price down. That is why a sunny afternoon often has cheap power.
  • In 2024, CAISO's day-ahead price averaged $41 per MWh. CAISO prices also change by place; we ignore that here.

✓SOURCE ISO New England, "How resources are selected and prices are set" (another grid operator, same idea) ✓SOURCE CAISO 2024 market report: $41 per MWh

Tomorrow: the real-time market and the "plant reset"

  • Real use never matches the plan exactly. Example (made-up numbers): the plan said 26,000 MW at 7 p.m. Real use was 26,800 MW. The 800 MW gap (3%) is bought in the real-time market.
  • What the reset is. Every 15 minutes and every 5 minutes, CAISO's computer checks real use and sends each plant a new target: "make a bit more" or "make a bit less". It is like a gas pedal that holds a steady speed.
  • Why. To keep supply equal to use every second, at the lowest cost. Plants that can move fast do the work: batteries, dams, some gas plants. Big gas plants cannot start fast. That is why the plan is made the day before. Solar can be turned down fast. That is curtailment (section 8).

✓SOURCE CAISO market processes: real-time market dispatches every 15 and 5 minutes

When the guess is wrong

Too high costs money. Too low wastes power.

Curtailment: solar turned down on purpose

  • In our period, 7.6% of the solar that could have been made was turned down. In April 2026: 24%.
  • Most happens in spring: lots of sun, little demand.
  • In 2024, 3.4 TWh of wind and solar was turned down in CAISO's area. 93% of it was solar.

Big solar farms vs rooftop solar

Same sun. Very different counting.

  • Rooftop = under 1 MW per system. California had about 20 GW of it in 2025. It made about 34 TWh, 37% of all U.S. rooftop solar.
  • About 2 million systems. About 1 in 6 California homes. Averaged over all systems (homes and businesses), about 9 kW each. A typical U.S. home system is smaller: 7.7 kW.
  • Since April 2023 the credit for sending extra power out is lower, so more homes add batteries.
  • Why the grid cannot see it. The utility's meter sits at the home's front door. It counts only what the home takes from the grid. So rooftop power looks like the home used less.
  • Why the official count leaves it out. The U.S. Energy Information Administration counts plants of 1 MW or more in its main power numbers. Rooftop systems are under 1 MW, so they are estimated separately and usually left out of the main count.

The scorecard: 4 guesses, 1 ruler

Each bar is 1 way to guess tomorrow's solar for each daylight hour. The bar shows how far off that guess was, on average. Shorter is better.

  • What "miss" means. The average gap between the guess and what happened, divided by all solar plant capacity. Example with made-up capacity: if plants can make 25,000 MW at most, a 5% miss is 1,250 MW off in an average daylight hour. =CALCULATED 5% x 25,000 MW = 1,250 MW
  • Bars 1 and 2 are free tricks anyone can do. Bar 2 exists because "yesterday" is not fully known at 10 a.m., so the fair copy is 2 days ago.
  • Bars 3 and 4 are the experts at CAISO. 2 versions, because solar is sometimes turned down on purpose (section 8). The meter then shows less than the sun could make. Bar 3 counts the meter as it is. Bar 4 adds the turned-down solar back, so CAISO looks better.
  • Our goal: at most 1.25 times CAISO's miss. That is 3.4% (1.25 x 2.7%) to 5.9% (1.25 x 4.7%): the orange band. =CALCULATED 1.25 x 2.73 = 3.4; 1.25 x 4.73 = 5.9

Our time range, and why

  • Why start Mar 8, 2024? The weather forecast archive is complete from that day. We need weather and solar data for the same hours.
  • Why end Oct 17, 2026? Our data plan names Oct 17 as "the last full day we use" but does not say why. Our best explanation: it is a cushion of about 10 days before the Oct 27 to 28 data pulls. What could go wrong, in plain words

7 things that could trip us up, and what we do about each.

1. The weather forecast is wrong

WHY We rely on a free forecast made by someone else. A bad forecast means a bad solar guess.

WHAT WE DO We use the forecast made 2 days ahead, test the 1-day forecast as a comparison, and report errors by weather type. Forecast sunshine already tracks real output well: 0.87 on a scale where 1.0 is perfect.

2. We have little history

WHY The weather archive starts Mar 8, 2024. That is 954 days. Each season shows up only 2 to 3 times.

WHAT WE DO Keep the model simple. Test only in time order, never shuffled.

3. No winter in the final exam

WHY The exam is the newest 191 days, Apr 10 to Oct 17, 2026. A real forecast only knows the past, so the test must be the newest days. That leaves out winter, when solar is lowest and days are shortest.

WHAT WE DO All 180 winter days sit in the practice period, so winter is learned and tested there (in 2 of the 4 practice tests, if they are the last 4 three-month blocks). We report winter results separately and say clearly they come from practice, not the final exam.

4. Turned-down solar hides the truth

WHY The meter counts what was delivered, not what the sun allowed. Up to 24% in a spring month.

WHAT WE DO Flag those hours. Report results with and without them.

5. Clocks do not match

WHY Our main solar count (from the U.S. Energy Information Administration) ran 1 hour behind the sun and the other sources until Dec 2, 2025. Since then it is on time. A 1-hour slip ruins results.

WHAT WE DO We compared it hour by hour with CAISO's own count, and with the true time of solar noon. Before Dec 2, 2025 the files line up only when we move our count back 1 hour (0.998 match). After that date we do not move it (0.998 match; moving it would drop the match to 0.81). All sources then use 1 clock (UTC). We do not know why the count changed, and the team still confirms the rule.

6. Missing data

WHY 226 of 22,896 hours (0.99%) have a gap: 129 weather hours, 72 CAISO plan hours and 25 solar count hours. The 129 weather hours are in April 2026, inside the exam period.

WHAT WE DO We do not invent values. Short gaps are filled, long gaps are dropped, and every choice is written down.

7. Rooftop solar is invisible, and solar keeps growing

WHY Rooftop solar shows up as lower demand. Big-farm capacity grew 24% in 2.5 years.

WHAT WE DO Divide errors by capacity so months compare fairly. State clearly that rooftop is not in our numbers.

Quick answers

7 questions people ask.

Why not just look at the sky?

1 sky covers a few miles. Solar farms are spread across a huge state. And the plan is made the day before, so we need to know today what tomorrow's sky will do. Weather forecast models do that.

Why not use last year's weather?

The sun's path repeats every year. Clouds do not. A sunny Aug 3 last year says nothing about tomorrow. What repeats is the time of day and the season, and our model already knows those.

Why not copy yesterday?

It works decently: an average miss of 4.9%. But it fails when the weather changes. It is the minimum score we must beat.

Why not just use CAISO's forecast?

That is the score we compare ourselves against. Our job is to see whether free public data can get close.

Why only solar, not total demand?

CAISO already forecasts total demand very well: within about 3% on our 4 sample days. Solar is the hard part.

Why California and not New York?

Short answer: California has the most solar, and its grid gives away the data we need for free. A bigger solar business means a better guess is worth more.

  • California is about 6 times bigger in solar. California: more than 49,000 MW of solar, rank 1 in the U.S. New York: 7,922 MW, rank 7. 49,000 ÷ 7,922 = about 6.
  • Solar matters more there. Solar makes more than 31% of California's electricity. In New York it is 7.56%. A miss in the solar guess hurts more where solar is bigger.
  • California is the bigger power market. In 2024 California made 214.2 TWh of electricity and New York made 129.0 TWh (all sources).
  • Free data and a score to beat. CAISO and the U.S. Energy Information Administration publish hourly numbers for free. CAISO also publishes its own day-ahead solar forecast. That is the score we try to match.

Why 10 a.m.?

Offers for tomorrow close at 10 a.m. Pacific the day before. Anything we use has to be known by then, or our results would look better than real life. CAISO publishes the plan at 1 p.m.
