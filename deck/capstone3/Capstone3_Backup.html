Three ordinary test days, forecast against actual

These are not our best or worst days. We picked the median-error day in each cloud group by a written rule. On the clear day our line sits a little under the actual peak and CAISO's sits above it. Backup: CAISO adjusted

Why the adjusted CAISO score is not a fair yardstick

What we checkedTurn-downs match CAISO's own over-forecast

CAISO measures turned-down solar against its own forecast. In hours with large turn-downs, the two numbers add up to 104%, correlation 0.89.

What it meansAdding it back removes most of CAISO's error

In the 777 heaviest hours, CAISO's error is 11.75% as published and 2.77% adjusted. So the adjusted score is low partly by construction.

So our headline comparison is CAISO as published, and we show adjusted as a second line. On the test, adjusted is 3.54%.

Checked on the 4,508 validation hours (FAQ Decision D27).

CAISO defines curtailment against its own forecast. When we add curtailed energy back to actual output, we are mostly adding back CAISO's own over-forecast. That is why we treat adjusted as a second line.
Backup: how the model works

20 inputs, all known at 10 a.m.

Weather forecast, 2 days before (10)

Day-2 forecasts of cloud cover, sunshine, temperature, humidity, wind and rain, plus a cloud index against a clear sky.

Time and sun (7)

Hour of day, hour and day of year as smooth curves, sun height, clear-sky sunshine.

Size and recent output (3)

Installed capacity that month, output 2 days earlier, average output on the day 2 days earlier.

Left out on purpose: output from 1 day earlier, CAISO's forecast, and day-1 weather, because they arrive after the 10 a.m. bid close.

FAQ Decision D28. Two assumptions are not verified: the 2-day-old weather forecast is available by 10 a.m., and EIA's values from 2 days earlier are published by then.

The model is a tree model, many small decision trees, each fixing the mistakes of the one before. All 20 inputs are known at 10 a.m. We left out anything that arrives later.

Backup: what the dollar figure means

About $120 million a year: a size, not a savings promise

Step 12.83 points

Error gap between the same-as-2-days-ago guess (7.27%) and our model (4.43%).

Step 23.0 TWh a year

One point of error is about 1.06 TWh a year (Capstone 2 numbers). 2.83 times 1.06.

Step 3About $122 million

At $41 per MWh, CAISO's 2024 average day-ahead price. 2.83 times $43 million a point.

It sizes the problem. It does not say how much money a grid operator would save, and we do not compute a dollar figure against CAISO's own forecast.

Source: Capstone 2 numbers file (deck/capstone2/CAPSTONE2_NUMBERS.md) and reports/reserve_test.csv.

The dollar figure is a size of the problem. From our Capstone 2 numbers, one point of error is about 1.06 terawatt-hours a year. At 41 dollars a megawatt-hour, that is about 43 million dollars a point.

Backup: limits and open items

What we have not checked or cannot show

  • No winter in the test. Winter was checked only in practice runs.
  • 2.6 years of history. Each month appears 2 or 3 times.
  • Two timing assumptions. The 2-day-old weather forecast is available by 10 a.m., and EIA's values from 2 days earlier are published by then. Neither is verified.
  • One capacity source. Errors are shares of EIA 860M nameplate, all CISO, and we name it next to every MW figure.
  • Turned-down solar is a rough series. It is above zero in 94% of hours, probably because CAISO defines it against its own forecast.
  • Forecast high on test. Bias +494 MW, cause only partly understood.

FAQ open items and Decision D31 (team repository).

These are the limits we know about. We would rather list them than have them found.
